Behavioral Responses to Air Quality Information: Implications for Optimal Policy

Air quality alerts change outdoor activity, but the behavioral response depends on recent alert history. I combine quasi-experimental evidence with a model of alert policy to study how these dynamics affect the welfare-maximizing alert threshold.

Air quality alerts can improve welfare by helping individuals avoid pollution exposure. However, lowering the alert threshold creates a tradeoff: issuing more warnings can encourage avoidance on additional days, but may also change the effectiveness of subsequent alerts. I develop a model of optimal air quality alert policy that accounts for these history-dependent behavioral responses. Determining the optimal threshold requires understanding both how alerts affect outdoor activity and how responsiveness varies with recent alert exposure. I estimate these effects using a fuzzy regression discontinuity design that exploits the jump in alert issuance at a forecasted air quality index (AQI) of 101, combining a panel of air quality forecasts, alert issuances, and high-frequency foot traffic data from six U.S. metropolitan areas. Near the threshold, receiving an alert reduces daily visits to outdoor recreational points of interest by approximately 9 percent. However, the effect depends strongly on recent alert history. There is no detectable response when no alerts have occurred in the previous seven days; alerts reduce visits by roughly 16 percent following one or two recent alerts, but the effect attenuates after three or more alerts. I then use these estimates to calibrate the model and conduct counterfactual simulations using data from New York City. The simulations place the welfare-maximizing alert threshold at an AQI of approximately 80, below the current threshold of 101. These results show that optimal warning policy depends not only on the risks communicated by alerts, but also on how repeated warnings shape the effectiveness of subsequent alerts.

Air Quality Forecasts in Practice (working title)

Air quality forecasts systematically differ from realized conditions, overpredicting AQI by approximately 9 points on average. This paper documents differences in the distributions and temporal dynamics of forecasted and realized AQI and investigates potential explanations for these discrepancies, including meteorological conditions and forecasting practices.

More about this project Hide project details

Air quality forecasts play an important role in communicating pollution risks to the public and informing decisions about outdoor activity. However, forecasted air quality may differ systematically from realized conditions. This paper examines the relationship between forecasted and realized Air Quality Index (AQI) values, with the objectives of documenting these differences and investigating their potential sources. Using daily air quality forecasts and observed AQI across U.S. metropolitan areas, I compare the distributions and temporal dynamics of forecasted and realized air quality. Preliminary descriptive results suggest that forecasts systematically overpredict realized AQI, with an average forecast error of approximately 9 AQI points. Additionally, the conditional distribution of realized AQI exhibits substantial dispersion, particularly at higher forecast levels. Forecasted and realized AQI also exhibit different patterns of serial correlation. The paper seeks to identify potential explanations for these differences, including meteorological conditions and features of the forecasting process. Understanding these discrepancies has implications for evaluating air quality forecasts and the effectiveness of forecast-based public health alerts.
